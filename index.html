What this actually is

Start here

A small computer — a Raspberry Pi, roughly the size of a deck of cards — sits on a shelf and watches the stock market all day. Every minute it asks: is anything happening that one of my five strategies cares about? When the answer is yes, it writes down a pretend trade: what it bought or sold, at what price, and why.

No real money is involved. Not a dollar. This is called paper trading — the market data is completely real and live, but the buying and selling happens only in a text file. If a strategy loses $1,000, nothing has actually been lost. If it makes $1,000, nobody gets paid.

That distinction is the whole point. The goal isn't to make money right now. It's to find out whether any of these ideas would make money, before risking anything real. Most trading ideas that sound clever turn out not to work, and the cheapest possible way to discover that is with fake money and honest bookkeeping.

Why share it

Most trading content online shows you the wins. This page shows you the whole ledger, including a strategy that lost 10% of its pretend account in a single afternoon. The failures are the useful part — they're where the actual learning is.

The market in 90 seconds

The absolute basics

A share is a small piece of ownership in a company. If a company is split into a billion shares and you own one, you own a billionth of it. Shares are bought and sold all day on an exchange, and the price is simply whatever the most recent buyer and seller agreed on.

Prices move because opinions change. Good news, bad news, someone big needing to sell in a hurry — every trade nudges the price. Over a day, a normal large company might move 1%. A small, thinly-traded one might move 40%.

Two things you need to know to follow the rest

You can bet in both directions. Going long is the familiar one: buy at $10, hope to sell at $12. Going short is the mirror image — you borrow a share you don't own, sell it at $10, and hope to buy it back at $8 to return it, pocketing the difference. Shorting is how you profit when something falls. It's also more dangerous, because a price can rise without limit, so a short can lose more than you put in.

Trading is never free. Every trade has a hidden cost. Buyers and sellers never quite meet at one price — there's always a small gap called the spread, and you pay it coming in and going out. Add slippage (the price moving between when you decide and when the order lands) and every round trip quietly costs you something. This sounds like a footnote. It isn't; see what we've learned.

The one idea behind all five

The core bet

All five strategies are versions of the same wager, called mean reversion: this price has moved too far, too fast, and it will snap back. Like a rubber band. Stretch it far enough and it pulls home.

The reference point they measure "too far" against is usually VWAP — the volume-weighted average price, which is roughly the average price everyone actually paid for that stock today. If a stock is trading well above its VWAP, most of today's buyers are already in profit and might take it. That's the theory.

The opposite bet also exists and is equally respectable: momentum — this is moving, so it'll keep moving. Both can be true depending on the day, which is exactly why this is hard. A rubber band snaps back, right up until the day it's actually a rocket.

The flaw we found in all of it

Every one of these strategies measured how far a price had moved and treated distance as proof of exhaustion. Distance is not exhaustion. A stock that has run 12% can easily run 20%. Realising this — and building a second layer that asks whether the move is actually losing energy — has been the main work of the last month.

The five strategies

What each one bets on

Each strategy keeps its own separate pretend account of $10,000, its own ledger, and its own rules. They don't share money and they don't consult each other. That way one bad idea can't contaminate the record of a good one.

Swing

Buy healthy companies on a bad day

The only slow one. Once each morning it looks for a solid, long-term-rising stock that has just had a sharp short-term drop, and buys it. It sells when the price recovers to its recent average, or after ten trading days, or if it falls too far.

ORB-fade

Breakouts are usually traps

It watches where a stock trades in the first thirty minutes — the "opening range." When the price later pushes out of that range, most traders read that as a breakout and chase it. This strategy bets the opposite: that the break fails and the price falls back inside. We have tested this one exhaustively and it does not work; see the scoreboard.

Spike-fade Parked

Short the stocks that have gone vertical

The wild one. It hunts small companies that are up 10% or more in a day and bets against them once they show signs of rolling over. When it's right the payoff is large. When it's wrong — which is often — the stock keeps squeezing higher and the loss is fast. It lost 9% of its account in one morning, so it has been parked: it still hunts and records what it would have done, but its account is frozen and it can't affect anything.

How one trade works, start to finish

A worked example

Here is a real one from the Spike-fade ledger, a stock we'll call LITX, on 12 August. It was the best trade of that day.

  1. Notice LITX is up more than 10% from where it opened. It goes on the watch list.
  2. Check it's tradeable — above $5 a share, and enough people are trading it that we could realistically get in and out.
  3. Wait for a crack. Not just "it's up a lot," but evidence the run is breaking: the price slips below the last fifteen minutes' floor and sits at least 1% below the day's high.
  4. Decide the exit before entering. The stop goes just above the day's high. If the price gets there, the idea was wrong and we're out. Nothing about this is negotiable later.
  5. Size the position from the risk, not the reward. The most this trade may lose is 1% of the account, about $100. The distance to the stop determines how many shares that allows — a wider stop means fewer shares. Every trade risks the same $100 regardless of the stock.
  6. Enter. Sell short 133 shares at $36.29.
  7. Manage. As the price falls, the stop follows it down, locking in progress. It never moves the other way.
  8. Exit at $34.62 when the trailing stop is hit. Gross profit $222, minus $4 in spread and slippage costs, so +$217.48 booked.
  9. Write it down — entry, exit, size, cost, profit, and the reason — so it can be argued with later.

That was a good trade. The same strategy took fifteen others that day and thirteen of them lost, which is the honest context for any single winning example. One trade tells you nothing. Be suspicious of anyone who shows you one.

How it avoids blowing up

Guardrails

Losing is normal; being ruined is a design failure. Five rules run underneath every strategy:

  • Fixed risk per trade. Every position is sized so a loss costs about the same small slice of the account. No single trade can matter much.
  • An exit decided in advance. The stop is set before entry, and moves only in the direction that protects profit.
  • A cooldown. After a losing trade in a stock, that stock is benched for a while. We added this after finding that most of the worst losses were the bot re-entering the same collapsing name again and again.
  • A daily cap on how often it can touch one stock. Two attempts, then it moves on.
  • A circuit breaker. If a strategy drops 8% from its starting balance, it halts and waits for a human. That's what happened to Spike-fade.

What we've learned

The useful part

Ignoring costs invents profits that don't exist

One strategy looked like a winner for weeks. Then we added realistic spread and slippage to the bookkeeping and the same trades came out at −$91. The "edge" had been the absence of costs the entire time. Any backtest that doesn't subtract trading costs is fiction.

Distance is not exhaustion

Every strategy assumed that a price which had moved a long way was ready to turn. Reviewing one bad day trade by trade, the pattern was brutal: of the trades where the bot sold short a bar that had just set a new high for the day, every single one lost. It was standing in front of a moving train and calling it a rubber band.

A safety check can accidentally forbid the trade

So we built a rule: don't bet against something that just made a new high. Sensible — and for two of the five strategies it silently blocked almost every trade, because those strategies enter at a new high by definition. The rule wasn't detecting a bad trade, it was contradicting the strategy's own reason for existing. Measuring it before switching it on is the only reason we caught that.

Small samples lie confidently

Thirty trades can show a beautiful win rate and mean nothing. Fade-the-open has 274 trades, which is starting to be worth something; Swing has 31, which is barely a rumour. Most trading claims you'll encounter are built on samples too small to support them.

Bugs can quietly hide the truth

For five days, Spike-fade appeared to be doing nothing at all. It had in fact traded 39 times and lost $1,007 — the display was reading the wrong ledger. Separately, a Wi-Fi dropout froze one strategy so it couldn't close its open positions for an entire day. With fake money those are embarrassments. With real money the second one is how people get hurt: the stop that can't fire is the one you were relying on.

What would change our minds

The bar

Before any of this touches real money, all of the following would have to be true, and none of them are yet:

  • A few hundred trades at minimum — not thirty.
  • Profitable after costs, measured on data the strategy was never tuned against.
  • Beating buy-and-hold, because that's the free alternative.
  • Tested through a falling market, not just a rising one. Every strategy here looks better than it is in a market that mostly goes up.
  • Losses that stay boring. A strategy that makes 20% and then loses 40% is not a strategy.

Realistically, most or all of these five will fail that bar and be retired. That's the expected outcome of testing ideas honestly, and it's cheaper to find out this way.

Glossary

Reference

Long / Short
Long: you profit if the price rises. Short: you borrow and sell first, and profit if it falls.
Paper trading
Recording trades with real market prices but imaginary money, to test an idea without risking anything.
VWAP
Volume-weighted average price — roughly the average price paid for a stock so far today. Used as the "fair" reference point.
Stop
A pre-set price at which a losing position is closed, decided before entering.
Spread & slippage
The unavoidable costs of trading: the gap between buying and selling price, and the price drifting before your order lands.
Mean reversion
The bet that an unusually large move will partly reverse.
Momentum
The opposite bet: that a move will continue.
Backtest
Running a strategy against historical data to see how it would have done. Easy to fool yourself with.
Win rate
The share of trades that made money. On its own it means little — the size of wins versus losses matters more.
SPY
A fund tracking the 500 largest US companies. The default "do nothing" benchmark every strategy is measured against.

This is not financial advice, and nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It's a record of an experiment with imaginary money, shared because the process is interesting and the failures are instructive. If you're thinking about trading real money, understand that most people who try it lose, and talk to someone licensed to advise you.
